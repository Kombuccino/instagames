# MiniFugg — Welcome Cover Art Style Catalog

Reusable visual-language catalog for MiniFugg welcome covers, posters, home concepts and other promotional mockups.

This document describes **art direction**, not implementation. For the collectible cover product rules, unlocks and motion contract, read `docs/WELCOME_ILLUSTRATIONS.md`. For image storage/import, read `docs/ASSET_PIPELINE.md`. For layer tuning, read `docs/PARALLAX_LAB.md`.

The goal is that a future conversation, illustrator or image model can read this file alone and recover the visual vocabulary developed during the TetraMindFck pilot without falling back to generic AI art.

---

## 1. Core artistic principle

MiniFugg games are tiny games presented as if they were lost, overly ambitious premium games from another era.

The welcome art should feel like a human illustrator, graphic designer or regional publisher **interpreted** the game and oversold its fantasy. The illustration can be more dramatic, beautiful, strange or prestigious than the actual gameplay. That contrast is intentional.

Useful internal manifesto:

> **Small games presented like great lost videogame fantasies.**

The cover should not look like a neutral screenshot or modern app-store card. It should feel like an object with an author: game box, magazine advertisement, illustrated poster, regional edition, print campaign, computer-game packaging, cultural reinterpretation, etc.

---

## 2. Shared MiniFugg spine

Different covers must not become one rigid template. Collection coherence should come from a few constants rather than identical composition.

For finished Fugg covers, normally preserve:

- portrait format, usually around 9:16;
- readable game title;
- one strong principal illustration or graphic idea;
- optional short slogan;
- discreet MiniFugg mark when useful;
- bottom CTA: **`SWIPE TO PLAY ↑`**;
- a visual idea that is truthful to the game's mechanic even when dramatically exaggerated;
- enough planned visual separation to support real raster parallax when animation is intended.

Do **not** require the same cartridge mockup, frame, sunset, title placement, color palette or composition across games.

---

## 3. Anti-generic-AI rules

The first experimental covers failed when they became too recognizably “AI generated.” Avoid these recurring defaults:

- glossy/plastic 3D rendering by default;
- generic cinematic key art;
- automatic purple/cyan neon or synthwave sunset;
- giant centered hero with decorative objects floating symmetrically around them;
- every square centimeter filled with detail;
- shallow fake film grain over an otherwise modern digital render;
- identical title/hero/CTA hierarchy on every game;
- excessive bloom, volumetric light and rim lighting;
- “look how much detail I can generate” compositions;
- generic American blockbuster/action-figure language when the concept does not call for it.

Prefer signs of authored human choices:

- an identifiable medium: gouache, acrylic, ink, airbrush, collage, screen print, offset print, cheap four-color print, photocopy, coarse halftone, paper cut, etc.;
- selective detail instead of detail everywhere;
- deliberate negative space;
- imperfect registration, worn ink, paper texture or hand-painted marks;
- purposeful cropping;
- asymmetry when useful;
- custom typographic hierarchy;
- limited or intentionally constrained palette;
- visual jokes, awkwardness or editorial choices that do not merely maximize spectacle.

A useful test: **could this plausibly have been commissioned, printed and art-directed by a small game publisher, magazine or regional distributor?**

# 4. Main style families

These are reusable families, not mandatory one-to-one templates. A game can use several of them as collectible variants.

## A — Franco-Belgian / European pulp illustration

### Intent

Expressive commercial illustration with the energy of late-80s/early-90s European game advertising, pulp magazines and bande-dessinée-adjacent print work. It should feel hand-made and characterful rather than like Hollywood key art.

### Visual language

- expressive faces and body language;
- readable physical situation or narrative moment;
- humor, grotesque exaggeration or psychological tension welcome;
- ink outlines, painted shadows, gouache/acrylic texture;
- rough brushwork and print imperfections;
- dramatic but not hyper-photorealistic anatomy;
- props chosen for storytelling rather than decoration;
- deep darks and strong local color, but palette remains intentional.

### Composition

- one dominant human/object situation;
- diagonals and explosive forms work well;
- title can feel hand-lettered or printed rather than perfectly typeset;
- background may be atmospheric, illustrative or partially abstract;
- visual focus should remain obvious at phone size.

### What makes it feel human

- slightly ugly/expressive faces are often better than beautiful ones;
- imperfect lettering;
- brush or ink texture visible;
- not every object rendered with equal precision;
- composition can contain comic exaggeration.

### Good fit

Dark comedy, bureaucracy, bodily/grotesque games, food violence, life/debt satire, character-heavy concepts.

Examples inside MiniFugg thinking: Crazy Papers, Debth of Life, Hari, Vlad, and TetraMindFck's `pulp-euro` interpretation.

### Avoid

- superhero movie poster finish;
- perfectly glossy digital painting;
- generic “epic hero in center” composition;
- photoreal skin and cinematic lens effects.

---

## B — European micro-computer box art

### Intent

A believable lost Amiga / Atari ST / DOS / CPC-era commercial cover or magazine advertisement without copying any single historical box.

Heroic and dramatic, but more eccentric, technical and graphic than contemporary Hollywood key art.

### Visual language

- painted/airbrushed illustration;
- CRTs, keyboards, technical diagrams, grids or machine interfaces when relevant;
- packaging-like hierarchy;
- strong title block;
- occasional frames, labels, publisher marks, pseudo-technical copy;
- dark ink/paint backgrounds with bright printed accents;
- visible paper/board wear and offset-print character.

### Composition

- strong vertical cover hierarchy;
- title may occupy a substantial top zone;
- central subject often framed by environmental or technical context;
- UI-like or schematic details can act as editorial decoration, but must belong to the imagined period;
- more structured than the pulp variant.

### What makes it feel human

- old commercial illustration logic instead of modern concept art;
- slightly awkward packaging typography;
- analogue airbrush/paint transitions;
- printed borders and wear;
- selective technical nonsense that looks designed rather than generated as random glyph soup.

### Good fit

Logic, puzzle, systems, machines, trains, numbers, arcade abstractions, retro-technology concepts.

Examples: TetraMindFck `micro-euro`, LineFugg, Train Fighter.

### Avoid

- modern cyberpunk UI;
- generic neon synthwave;
- glossy RGB gaming-PC aesthetic;
- fake oldness created only by adding a noise layer.

---

## C — Eastern-European / cultural graphic poster

### Intent

The most editorial, symbolic and authored family. Inspired by broad historical poster traditions, constructivist geometry, political/cultural print language and experimental graphic design — not by copying a particular poster or artist.

This is often the strongest antidote to generic AI imagery.

### Visual language

- reduced palette;
- bold flat forms;
- paper stock visible;
- offset misregistration, coarse ink, halftone or collage texture;
- symbolic rather than literal representation;
- geometric fragmentation;
- silhouettes, cut shapes and visual metaphors;
- typography treated as a graphic object.

### Composition

- strong negative space;
- asymmetry and tension;
- large blocks of one or two colors;
- scale changes can be extreme;
- one memorable symbolic idea instead of many literal objects;
- title can be integrated into geometry rather than placed above an illustration.

### What makes it feel human

- restraint;
- deliberate omissions;
- strange proportions;
- graphic decisions that are not “pretty” in a generic sense;
- visible print logic.

### Good fit

Abstract games, logic, mental overload, social satire, bureaucracy, existential themes, games that benefit from a striking poster more than a literal scene.

TetraMindFck's `graphic-poster` is the reference direction.

### Avoid

- turning “Eastern European” into random Cyrillic decoration;
- adding historical propaganda clichés without conceptual reason;
- overcomplicating the image until the graphic restraint disappears.

---

## D — Foreign / regional edition

### Intent

Imagine that the same game was locally reinterpreted by another publishing/editorial culture. This is **not translation pasted onto the same illustration**. The local edition may rethink hierarchy, title treatment, composition, color rhythm, framing and symbolism.

A finished Fugg can use this as its fourth official variant, with additional regional editions becoming rarer bonus covers later.

The cultural edition should feel like a plausible local studio or distributor interpreted the material.

### Universal rule

Avoid tourist caricature and instant “exotic” kitsch. Do not reduce a country to flags, temples, calligraphy, dragons, cherry blossoms, propaganda symbols, etc. Cultural cues should primarily come from **editorial/layout/print conventions**, not souvenir iconography.

---

# 5. Regional variation notes

## D1 — Japanese edition

### Direction

Late-80s/early-90s Japanese game packaging, magazine advertising and print/editorial energy interpreted broadly.

### Useful traits

- more energetic vertical rhythm;
- denser information hierarchy can be acceptable;
- bold Japanese typography interacting with Latin game title;
- inserts, seals, edition markers or side copy where composition benefits;
- manga/editorial line energy where appropriate;
- red/black/cream, primary-color or strong limited palettes can work well;
- off-register print, aged paper or commercial print texture;
- character can be drawn more graphically rather than rendered cinematically.

### TetraMindFck interpretation

Mental overload can become an aggressive vertical eruption of tetrominoes, numbers and fragments through the character's head, with Japanese editorial callouts acting as part of the print design.

### Avoid

- random kanji as decorative texture;
- obligatory Mount Fuji, rising sun, torii, sakura, samurai, anime eyes, etc.;
- simply translating the European cover without changing the design logic.

---

## D2 — Chinese edition

### Direction

A structured, symbolic or print-led reinterpretation. The exact visual language should depend on the chosen historical/commercial period rather than a generic “Chinese” aesthetic.

### Useful traits

- strong hierarchy and framing;
- red/black/cream or other controlled print palettes when appropriate, but not mandatory;
- symbolic geometry and repeated modules;
- poster-like relation between text and image;
- potentially more formal/structured composition than the Japanese variant;
- print texture, paper stock and graphic symbolism can dominate over painterly realism.

### Avoid

- tourist-poster dragons, lanterns, pagodas or pseudo-calligraphy by default;
- generic propaganda pastiche unless the concept actually justifies that historical reference;
- meaningless Chinese-looking glyphs.

---

## D3 — Korean edition

### Direction

Potentially sharper, more technical, nervous and modern in rhythm while retaining print/editorial credibility.

### Useful traits

- strong Hangul blocks as graphic architecture;
- dense but highly organized information;
- hard cropping;
- technical diagrams and commercial graphic energy;
- contrast between clean modern structure and distressed analogue print can be effective.

### Avoid

- assuming “Korean” means contemporary K-pop/neon aesthetics;
- copying modern brand/UI tropes when the target era is older.

---

## D4 — Eastern-European regional edition

This can overlap family C but push the “foreign edition” fiction further through local typography, edition marks and regional print logic.

Useful when a game wants an especially conceptual, strange or austere collectible variant.

# 6. Recommended four-cover system

The strongest current MiniFugg set is:

1. **Hero / narrative** — often family A: tells the game's fantasy through a memorable character/situation.
2. **Micro-computer / commercial** — family B: believable old game packaging or advertisement.
3. **Graphic / editorial** — family C: conceptual poster with more restraint and authorship.
4. **Regional edition** — family D: Japan, China, Korea, Eastern Europe or another culture chosen specifically for the game.

Do not force every game to interpret these identically. The purpose is variation, not four templates.

Extra regional covers can exist as rare bonus unlocks instead of replacing one another.

---

# 7. MiniFugg house signature without a house template

If a cover needs to feel recognizably MiniFugg while remaining unique, combine:

- **B's readability and commercial-cover hierarchy**;
- **C's restraint, graphic authorship and negative space**;
- **A's handmade character and emotional energy**.

This combination is preferable to enforcing a single universal visual style.

The brand should feel like a collection curated by the same eccentric publisher, not like every game was painted by the same illustrator.

---

# 8. Background rule

Backgrounds should be designed per game and per variant.

Do not reuse a universal synthwave sunset, neon void, abstract purple gradient or identical cartridge shelf.

A background can be:

- a real illustrated environment;
- paper/print field;
- graphic negative space;
- technical grid;
- city/interior;
- collage;
- symbolic shape system;
- almost empty.

The correct background is whatever strengthens the particular cover's editorial idea.

---

# 9. Typography rule

Typography is part of the art, not an afterthought.

Prefer:

- period-appropriate display lettering;
- hand-painted or inked title treatment when relevant;
- compressed grotesks, slab faces, technical type, vernacular display or regional scripts appropriate to the concept;
- imperfect print and registration;
- hierarchy with a few decisive sizes.

Avoid:

- generic modern gaming-logo chrome;
- every title rendered as glossy extruded 3D;
- tiny explanatory text everywhere;
- random unreadable pseudo-language.

For cultural editions, native-language copy must be treated as real typography, not decorative glyph texture.

---

# 10. Motion / parallax art planning

The artwork should be conceived with animation in mind, not flattened first and “fixed” with CSS later.

When motion is planned, design real raster planes such as:

1. background/environment;
2. midground event/props/explosion/geometric elements;
3. foreground character/main object;
4. overlay/title/CTA.

Not every cover needs identical layers. A graphic poster may use paper + geometric forms + silhouette + typography. A micro-computer cover may use room/CRT + screen/tetromino effects + character + title.

Autonomous motions should come from the subject:

- **vibrate** — panic, nervous character, unstable machine;
- **float** — suspended objects, dreams, zero-gravity, drifting blocks;
- **breathe** — explosion/glow/pressure field slowly swelling;
- **drift** — smoke, dust, clouds, paper, fog;
- **sway** — signs, hanging objects, plants, ropes;
- restrained parallax — depth between real raster planes.

CSS may add lighting/vignette/glow effects, but should not invent the important graphical content.

# 11. Prompt / brief recipe for future image creation

When briefing an illustrator or image model, describe the cover in this order:

### 1. Object

“Standalone portrait game poster/cover, full frame, no mockup, no dashboard, no concept sheet.”

This matters because image models often drift into presentation boards if the production process has been discussed recently.

### 2. Game truth

State the actual mechanic and the key fantasy to exaggerate.

Example structure:

“Falling tetromino pieces + arithmetic mental overload + a person whose mind is visibly breaking under the pressure.”

### 3. Style family

Choose A, B, C or D and describe the medium/era, not a living artist.

### 4. Composition

Define one strong subject, title hierarchy, negative space/density and important directional movement.

### 5. Material

Name the physical visual medium: ink/gouache, airbrush box art, offset poster, screen print, worn paper, collage, etc.

### 6. Anti-patterns

Explicitly exclude glossy 3D, generic cinematic key art, synthwave, concept sheet, interface, mockup or whatever is inappropriate.

### 7. Required text

Always supply the exact title and `SWIPE TO PLAY ↑` if it belongs on the final master.

### 8. Parallax foresight

Before final production, identify what will become background / midground / subject / overlay so hidden areas can be reconstructed cleanly.

---

# 12. Applying these styles outside game covers

The same families can be used for:

- MiniFugg global home variations;
- promotional posters;
- fake magazine advertisements;
- social artwork;
- event/season cards;
- creator profile art;
- launch screens;
- collectible gallery cards;
- experimental mockups.

When used outside a game cover, remove only the product-specific rules that do not apply (`SWIPE TO PLAY`, unlock score, etc.) while preserving the chosen visual language.

For the global MiniFugg home, several rotating interpretations are preferable to one generic landing-page illustration.

---

# 13. Reference pilot: TetraMindFck

TetraMindFck established the first working comparison set:

- `pulp-euro` — expressive European pulp / hand-painted psychological overload;
- `micro-euro` — European late-80s/early-90s micro-computer commercial cover;
- `graphic-poster` — Eastern-European/editorial graphic poster;
- regional edition — Japanese direction selected as the strongest first cultural candidate, with Chinese direction also worth preserving as a possible rarer edition.

The important discovery was that **all three initial visual families could be excellent simultaneously**. MiniFugg therefore should not choose one universal art style; it should treat variant diversity as part of the collectible system.

---

## 14. Short restart brief

If a future conversation needs the entire direction in one paragraph, use this:

> Create MiniFugg welcome art like lost premium game packaging or illustrated advertising from the late 1980s/early 1990s. The work must feel visibly authored and printed, not like generic glossy AI key art. Use a controlled family of variants: expressive Franco-Belgian/European pulp illustration; believable European micro-computer box art; restrained Eastern-European/editorial graphic poster; and a genuine regional reinterpretation such as Japanese or Chinese publishing language. Preserve paper, ink, gouache, airbrush, offset, halftone, collage and registration imperfections where appropriate; use deliberate negative space and selective detail; vary the background and composition per game; avoid universal synthwave/neon, plastic 3D and centered cinematic hero templates. For a Fugg cover, keep the exact game title readable and use `SWIPE TO PLAY ↑`. If animation is intended, plan real raster background/midground/subject/overlay layers before integration.
